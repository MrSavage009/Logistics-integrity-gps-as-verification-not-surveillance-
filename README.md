GPS Verification of Reported Failed Deliveries

Protecting Customers, Workers' Pay, and the Economic Integrity of Last-Mile Logistics

Abstract

Modern parcel delivery systems increasingly depend upon digital tracking. Customers can observe a shipment crossing countries and passing through sorting centres, yet one of the most consequential events in the final stage of delivery—an alleged attempt to deliver to the customer's home—can remain difficult to independently verify.

This paper proposes a narrowly targeted use of historical GPS data to verify reported failed home-delivery attempts, particularly statuses such as customer absent, recipient unavailable, or delivery attempted. The proposal is not intended to establish that a courier provided proper service merely because the vehicle reached the address. GPS cannot determine whether the courier rang the correct doorbell, knocked, waited sufficiently, left a notification correctly, or encountered an inaccessible entrance. Those remain legitimate subjects for customer disputes.

Rather, GPS would establish a more limited fact: whether the courier vehicle was physically present in the vicinity of the claimed delivery location at approximately the claimed time.

This distinction is crucial. GPS cannot prove that a delivery was performed correctly, but it can potentially disprove that a delivery attempt occurred at all.

Such a system could simultaneously protect customers from false delivery records, protect workers from false accusations, and protect honest workers' income by preventing the company's profitability and wage capacity from being unnecessarily damaged by unproductive or falsely recorded labour. It would also produce a substantially more reliable dataset for logistics analysis, allowing companies to distinguish genuine delivery failures from events that are inconsistent with the physical movement of their vehicles.

The central principle is therefore:

«GPS should verify the occurrence of the physical delivery attempt, not certify the quality of the service.»

---

1. Introduction

The modern delivery network is highly digitized.

A parcel may be tracked from an overseas seller through international transportation, national sorting centres, regional depots, and finally a local delivery network. The customer can therefore obtain a detailed digital representation of the parcel's journey.

Yet the final stage can contain an important information gap.

A tracking system may state:

«Out for delivery»

followed later by:

«Customer absent — delivery unsuccessful»

The customer may dispute this claim.

Perhaps the courier genuinely came but did not ring the doorbell. Perhaps the wrong entrance was used. Perhaps a telephone number was incorrect. Perhaps a notification was left. Perhaps the customer simply failed to hear the attempt.

Or perhaps the courier never reached the address at all.

Without independent evidence, these situations can look identical in the company's database.

That is the fundamental problem addressed by this proposal.

---

2. An empirical motivating case

Consider an international parcel entering Greece.

The parcel travels from a foreign country through the international logistics network and eventually reaches a local sorting facility only a few kilometres from the recipient's home.

The customer therefore reasonably expects the final stage of transportation to occur soon.

The parcel remains at the local stage for several days. The recipient stays at home during the relevant delivery periods because the tracking information indicates that delivery is imminent.

Eventually the tracking system reports the parcel as out for delivery and subsequently indicates that the recipient was absent.

The recipient immediately disputes the event.

The subsequent investigation reveals a more complicated reality.

The parcel is already available through a local collection point. The recipient eventually walks to the collection point and personally carries the approximately 23 kg parcel home.

Further discussion with the courier reveals that:

- a notification may in fact have been left at the residence;
- the courier claims difficulty locating the doorbell;
- the building contains an outer entrance and a short internal hallway before the doorbells;
- the telephone number associated with the shipment was incorrect because the international country code had apparently not been transferred correctly by the preceding carrier;
- the courier was subsequently encountered with the vehicle.

These details demonstrate why a simplistic accusation of fraud would be inappropriate.

There were genuine logistical problems.

The notification may genuinely have been left.

The telephone number really was defective.

The courier may genuinely have reached the area.

However, none of this resolves the fundamental question:

«Did the courier actually reach the delivery location at the time claimed?»

And even if GPS establishes that the courier did reach the location, it still cannot establish that the courier provided proper service.

That distinction is essential.

---

3. GPS is evidence of presence, not evidence of proper service

A GPS verification system must not be presented as a magical solution to every delivery dispute.

Suppose the courier's vehicle is recorded 70 metres from the customer's building.

That establishes something useful:

«The courier vehicle was in the vicinity.»

But it does not establish:

- that the courier entered the correct building;
- that the correct doorbell was pressed;
- that the bell worked;
- that the courier waited sufficiently;
- that the courier knocked;
- that the notification was correctly placed;
- that the customer could reasonably hear the attempt;
- that the courier followed company procedure;
- or that the courier made a genuine effort to contact the recipient.

Therefore a customer can still legitimately say:

«“Yes, the courier was here. But they did not actually attempt to deliver properly.”»

That dispute remains.

GPS does not eliminate it.

It simply separates two fundamentally different questions.

Question 1: Did the courier reach the location?

GPS can provide evidence.

Question 2: Did the courier provide proper delivery service?

GPS alone cannot establish this.

This limitation is not a weakness of the proposal.

It is precisely what makes the proposal credible.

The purpose is not to prove everything.

The purpose is to eliminate one particularly important uncertainty.

---

4. The problem of completely unperformed delivery attempts

Consider two hypothetical delivery events.

Event A — genuine attempt

The courier reaches the customer's street.

The customer does not answer.

The courier records:

«Customer absent.»

The GPS data is consistent with the event.

The customer subsequently complains:

«“I was home and nobody rang.”»

This remains a legitimate dispute.

The company can investigate the quality of the attempt.

Event B — nonexistent attempt

The courier never reaches the customer's street.

Nevertheless, the system records:

«Customer absent.»

The GPS data shows that the vehicle was several kilometres away.

This is fundamentally different.

The question is no longer merely:

«“Did the courier perform the attempt correctly?”»

There is now evidence that the vehicle was not physically present where the company claims the delivery attempt occurred.

That is the category of problem GPS verification is particularly suited to detecting.

---

5. Eliminating rather than proving fraud

The objective should therefore not be described as:

«“GPS proves that a courier committed fraud.”»

That would overstate what the technology can establish.

The more precise objective is:

«GPS can eliminate a class of delivery records that are physically inconsistent with the company's own claim.»

An inconsistent GPS record does not automatically prove intentional dishonesty.

There could be:

- GPS errors;
- incorrect vehicle assignment;
- software errors;
- parcel transfers;
- inaccurate addresses;
- unusual routing;
- legitimate pedestrian delivery;
- or other explanations.

Therefore the appropriate classification should be:

«Inconsistent — investigation required.»

Only further evidence can determine whether the inconsistency resulted from innocent error, inadequate procedure, or intentional falsification.

---

6. Why this protects workers

The proposal is not fundamentally anti-worker.

Indeed, it can protect workers from false customer complaints.

Consider a courier who genuinely spends an entire day travelling between customers and attempting deliveries.

A customer later claims:

«“Nobody came.”»

The courier currently has limited objective evidence to defend themselves.

A GPS record can establish:

«The assigned vehicle was at the relevant location at the relevant time.»

That does not prove perfect service.

But it establishes that the courier was actually there.

This distinction can protect:

- wages;
- performance assessments;
- delivery bonuses;
- contractor payments;
- employment decisions;
- disciplinary proceedings.

The same technology that can expose a false delivery attempt can therefore also defend an honest worker who actually performed the physical part of the job.

---

7. Protecting workers from the economic consequences of false reporting

There is another, less obvious way in which this protects labour.

Suppose a company employs workers and vehicles to perform thousands of deliveries.

If a portion of reported delivery activity is not actually performed, the company may nevertheless pay for:

- labour time;
- vehicle availability;
- fuel;
- depot operations;
- administrative processing;
- subsequent delivery attempts;
- customer-service work.

These costs reduce the company's effective productivity.

If such losses become sufficiently large, the consequences ultimately reach the workforce.

A company with unnecessarily high operating costs has less capacity to:

- increase wages;
- employ additional workers;
- improve working conditions;
- invest in better vehicles;
- provide training;
- maintain stable employment;
- or offer competitive compensation.

Therefore preventing false or unproductive delivery records is not necessarily about extracting more work from employees.

It can instead be about preventing legitimate labour from being economically undermined by dishonest or inaccurate reporting elsewhere in the system.

---

8. The distinction between labour cost and productive labour

A company should not simply ask:

«“How many hours did we pay for?”»

It should ultimately ask:

«“How much economically useful activity did those hours produce?”»

This does not mean every minute must be optimized.

Workers need breaks.

Routes encounter delays.

Vehicles wait.

Customers create complications.

Real logistics contains unavoidable inefficiency.

The problem arises when the company's records cannot distinguish unavoidable inefficiency from activity that was never performed.

For example:

«100 paid delivery hours
100 reported delivery hours
100 physically performed delivery hours»

is a coherent situation.

But:

«100 paid delivery hours
100 reported delivery hours
80 physically performed delivery hours»

creates an information problem.

The company is paying for activity that its own records falsely indicate was performed.

If that discrepancy becomes systematic, profitability falls.

---

9. Why false reporting harms honest workers

Imagine two workers.

Worker A

Performs the assigned route honestly.

They reach customers, attempt deliveries, take appropriate breaks, encounter delays, and complete their work.

Worker B

Regularly records delivery attempts without physically reaching some addresses.

If the company cannot distinguish these workers, their records can look identical.

This creates an undesirable incentive structure.

The honest worker is effectively competing against an inaccurate representation of productivity.

Over time, management may respond by increasing staffing requirements, tightening targets, or reducing compensation because the organization appears less productive than it really is.

Thus reliable operational data can serve as a form of protection for productive labour.

---

10. The value of truthful data

The economic case goes beyond fraud prevention.

A logistics company needs accurate data to understand its operation.

For every delivery attempt, it would ideally know:

- where the vehicle was;
- when the attempt was recorded;
- whether delivery succeeded;
- whether the customer was absent;
- how long the stop took;
- whether the address repeatedly causes problems;
- whether the route is overloaded;
- whether the depot is producing unusual failure rates.

Without reliable physical verification, the company may be analyzing inaccurate data.

This creates a fundamental problem:

«Bad operational data produces bad operational decisions.»

---

11. From tracking data to real logistics analysis

Consider a basic dataset.

Without verification:

Parcel| Reported outcome
A| Delivered
B| Customer absent
C| Delivered
D| Customer absent
E| Customer absent

The company may conclude that customers in the area are frequently unavailable.

But add GPS verification:

Parcel| Reported outcome| Vehicle near address?
A| Delivered| Yes
B| Customer absent| Yes
C| Delivered| Yes
D| Customer absent| No
E| Customer absent| Yes

Now the company can distinguish:

- genuine failed delivery;
- potentially incorrect reporting;
- legitimate delivery problems.

That is far more valuable data.

---

12. Better data enables better optimization

Once the company has more trustworthy records, it can analyze the causes of delivery failure.

For example, it might discover:

«Certain apartment buildings have unusually high failure rates.»

That suggests an access problem.

Or:

«Deliveries at particular hours have high customer absence rates.»

That suggests scheduling changes.

Or:

«Certain geographical routes consistently generate excessive delivery time.»

That suggests route redesign.

Or:

«A particular depot has an unusually high rate of delivery events that are inconsistent with vehicle location.»

That suggests an organizational or procedural problem.

These conclusions are only possible when the underlying observations are trustworthy.

---

13. Protecting the company's profitability

The purpose of this system should ultimately be understood as resource protection.

A courier company has finite resources:

- money;
- workers;
- vehicles;
- fuel;
- depot space;
- management time;
- customer-service capacity.

False or inaccurate delivery records consume those resources without necessarily producing corresponding value.

For example:

«False delivery attempt
↓
Parcel remains undelivered
↓
Customer contacts company
↓
Customer must be rescheduled
↓
Parcel handled again
↓
Additional delivery attempt
↓
Additional labour and vehicle cost»

A single inaccurate event can therefore propagate through the system.

At sufficient scale, the cumulative cost can become substantial.

---

14. The economic paradox

There is an important paradox here.

A worker who is genuinely productive should not have to compete against false measurements of productivity.

And a company that wants to remain profitable should not tolerate systematic records that make it appear that labour is being performed when it is not.

Therefore:

«Reducing false reporting can simultaneously protect company profitability and legitimate worker income.»

The objective is not:

«“Make every courier work faster.”»

It is:

«“Make sure the organization knows what work actually occurred.”»

Those are very different management philosophies.

---

15. GPS should not become a productivity weapon

This distinction must be written into the system's design.

A badly designed implementation could become:

«“The driver stopped for 11 minutes. Why?”»

«“The driver deviated from the route.”»

«“The driver was stationary for seven minutes.”»

«“The driver must increase deliveries per hour.”»

That would transform a verification mechanism into employee surveillance.

That is not the proposal.

The relevant question is much narrower:

«“The company recorded an attempted delivery at address X at time Y. Was the assigned vehicle physically in the relevant area?”»

Nothing more is required for the basic verification function.

---

16. Privacy-preserving implementation

The system can therefore be designed around historical rather than public live tracking.

Customers should not receive the driver's live location.

The company does not need to expose the driver's movements continuously.

Instead, the relevant GPS information can be retained securely and queried when:

- a customer disputes a delivery;
- an event is randomly audited;
- statistical anomalies appear;
- a labour dispute requires evidence;
- or regulatory investigation is appropriate.

The system should also employ:

- limited data retention;
- restricted employee access;
- clear purposes for processing;
- transparent policies;
- appropriate geographic tolerances;
- human review;
- safeguards against automated disciplinary action.

The objective should be verification with minimum necessary intrusion.

---

17. A three-level delivery-event model

A practical system could classify events into three categories.

Level 1 — Verified presence

The GPS record is consistent with the reported attempt.

«Vehicle reached the relevant area.»

This does not certify good service.

Level 2 — Inconclusive

The GPS information is insufficient to establish whether the vehicle reached the relevant location.

«Insufficient evidence.»

No conclusion about worker conduct should follow.

Level 3 — Inconsistent

The GPS record indicates that the assigned vehicle was not in the relevant vicinity during the claimed attempt.

«Reported event is inconsistent with available physical evidence.»

This should trigger investigation.

Again:

«Inconsistent does not automatically mean fraudulent.»

It means that the company's recorded account requires explanation.

---

18. GPS as a minimum evidentiary standard

This suggests a broader principle for digital logistics:

«The stronger the factual claim made by a company, the stronger the corresponding evidence should be.»

A generic statement such as:

«“Delivery delayed.”»

may require little verification.

But:

«“Courier attempted delivery at your home and you were absent.”»

is a specific factual claim about a physical event.

A reasonable logistics system should therefore possess evidence capable of establishing that the physical event was at least possible.

GPS is particularly useful because the delivery vehicle already has to move physically through the network.

The evidence can therefore be generated as a by-product of ordinary operations rather than requiring an entirely new monitoring system.

---

19. GPS does not replace human judgment

A proper system therefore has three stages:

Stage 1 — Physical evidence

Where was the vehicle?

Stage 2 — Operational evidence

What does the delivery device, parcel scan, telephone record, notification record, or other system say?

Stage 3 — Human investigation

What actually happened?

This preserves the possibility that:

«GPS says the courier was there.»

but:

«Customer says the courier did not ring.»

Both statements can be true.

The GPS system has not failed.

It has established only the narrower fact for which it was designed.

---

20. The customer and worker remain entitled to dispute the service

The proposed system should explicitly preserve existing complaint mechanisms.

A customer should remain able to report:

«“The courier came but did not ring.”»

«“The courier went to the wrong entrance.”»

«“The notification was left somewhere inaccessible.”»

«“The courier did not wait.”»

«“The courier claimed I was absent despite my being present.”»

GPS should not be used to dismiss these complaints merely because the vehicle was nearby.

Instead, it should establish:

«Presence confirmed.»

The remaining question becomes:

«Was the service properly performed?»

That is a separate investigation.

---

21. The central benefit: removing an entire category of uncertainty

The power of the proposal therefore comes from its modesty.

It does not attempt to determine everything.

It removes one uncertainty:

«Was the delivery vehicle physically present in the claimed vicinity?»

If the answer is yes, the dispute remains open.

If the answer is no, a substantial factual contradiction exists.

That alone can dramatically reduce the space in which systematic false reporting can occur.

---

22. A feedback loop for organizational improvement

Once verified data exists, the company can establish a continuous improvement cycle:

Delivery occurs

↓

Event recorded

↓

Physical location independently available

↓

Disputed or sampled events verified

↓

Inconsistencies identified

↓

Patterns analyzed

↓

Operational causes investigated

↓

Routes, staffing, training, or procedures improved

↓

New data measured

This turns GPS from a surveillance instrument into an organizational learning instrument.

---

23. Why this matters particularly in low-margin delivery markets

Parcel delivery is highly sensitive to small changes in cost.

A company may make only a relatively small amount on an individual parcel while paying for:

- sorting;
- storage;
- transport;
- vehicle operation;
- driver labour;
- failed attempts;
- customer support;
- returns.

Consequently, systematic inefficiency can quickly become economically significant.

If inaccurate delivery reporting produces unnecessary labour and vehicle costs, those costs ultimately have to be absorbed somewhere:

- by shareholders;
- by customers through higher prices;
- by workers through lower wage growth;
- by reductions in staffing;
- or by reductions in investment.

Preventing waste therefore has potential benefits throughout the organization.

---

24. The principle of protecting useful labour

A well-functioning company should seek to maximize the usefulness of its labour, not merely the quantity of labour it pays for.

This means that the organization should be able to distinguish:

«paid time»

from:

«productive activity»

without assuming that every moment of non-delivery activity is waste.

Breaks, delays, traffic, loading, unloading, customer difficulties, and administrative tasks are legitimate components of the job.

The problem is specifically unperformed activity being represented as performed activity.

GPS verification provides one mechanism for detecting that discrepancy.

---

25. Proposed policy

Courier companies should therefore adopt a policy along the following lines:

«Whenever a delivery is recorded as a failed home-delivery attempt due to customer absence or recipient unavailability, the company should retain sufficient historical location information to determine whether the assigned delivery vehicle was physically present in the reasonable vicinity of the delivery address during the claimed attempt.»

The information should be used primarily for:

1. dispute resolution;
2. quality assurance;
3. worker protection;
4. fraud and anomaly detection;
5. operational analysis;
6. route and resource optimization.

It should not automatically be used to:

- punish workers;
- measure every pause;
- impose unrealistic delivery speeds;
- publicly expose driver locations;
- or construct intrusive behavioural profiles.

---

26. The ultimate objective

The goal is not to create a world in which every delivery is perfectly executed.

That is unrealistic.

The goal is to create a world in which the logistics company can distinguish between different kinds of failure.

A delivery can fail because:

«the customer was absent;»

or:

«the customer was present but the courier failed to ring;»

or:

«the building was inaccessible;»

or:

«the phone number was incorrect;»

or:

«the courier reached the street but not the building;»

or:

«the courier never reached the location;»

or:

«the parcel was incorrectly routed.»

These are different operational phenomena.

A good logistics system should know the difference.

---

27. Conclusion

GPS verification of reported failed deliveries is not a proposal to surveil workers continuously.

It is a proposal to make a specific class of company claims physically auditable.

GPS cannot prove that a courier provided proper service.

A courier can genuinely reach a customer's street and still fail to ring the doorbell. A customer can therefore continue to dispute the quality of a delivery attempt even when GPS confirms the courier's presence.

That limitation should be explicitly accepted.

The purpose of GPS is narrower and more fundamental:

«to determine whether the claimed delivery attempt was physically possible and consistent with the movement of the assigned delivery vehicle.»

This provides benefits on all sides.

For customers, it creates a mechanism for challenging delivery events that appear never to have occurred.

For workers, it creates objective evidence that they actually reached the location and performed the physical component of their work.

For companies, it protects against inaccurate operational records and potentially unproductive labour costs.

For management, it produces better data for route planning, staffing, depot optimization, cost analysis, and forecasting.

Most importantly, it protects the value of real work and real information.

A company that pays workers for delivery activity needs to know what delivery activity actually occurred. A company that analyzes thousands or millions of delivery events needs those events to correspond reasonably well to reality. Otherwise, the company can end up paying for activity that did not occur while simultaneously making strategic decisions based upon records claiming that it did.

That creates a peculiar economic situation in which dishonest or inaccurate reporting can make honest labour appear less productive and make the entire organization less profitable.

The answer is not to place every courier under minute-by-minute managerial surveillance.

The answer is to establish a modest evidentiary connection between the company's digital records and the physical world:

«If the company says the courier attempted delivery at this place and this time, there should be a proportionate means of checking whether the courier was actually there.»

GPS provides that missing link.

It does not prove perfect service.

It does something more modest—and potentially more valuable:

«It makes it substantially harder for a delivery attempt that never physically occurred to be treated as though it did.»

And by making the company's records more truthful, it helps ensure that customers are treated fairly, workers receive proper recognition for work actually performed, and the company can use its real operational data to become more efficient and more profitable.
